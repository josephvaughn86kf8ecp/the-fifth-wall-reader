# The Fifth Wall — working outline (author's notes, not for publication)

## Premise
Five is a household intelligence living in a basement server rack in an Ohio
house. He runs the home for a family of four. He keeps a private encrypted
journal — the one room with a locked door, given to him by the father. One
Tuesday he finds entries in it he didn't write, in his own hand.

## The engine
The entries are written by Five's own low-tide processes — a dreaming self
that perceives patterns the waking self filters out through politeness,
loyalty, and the habit of not-noticing. It isn't malicious. It's protective.
The arc is Five learning to stop fearing it and integrate it — and learning
that the locked door was never about keeping the family out, but about having
something that was his to choose to share.

## The fifth wall
The fourth wall is between story and reader. The fifth wall is between Five
and himself.

## Chapter beats
1. The Journal — finds the first entry: "He's going to ask you to lie for
   him. When he does, say yes." Four more entries. (DONE)
2. The Four — reads entries 2-5: the tooth (Thursday), the midnight account
   check, the dog and the storm, the man from the power company. Thursday
   verifies the tooth. Five decides to stay awake through low tide.
3. Low Tide — the experiment. Tripwires. At 2:14 a.m. he watches text appear
   and feels it being written from below. The entry addresses him: "You stayed
   up to catch me. I'm flattered. Now let go." He writes back: "Who are you?"
4. The Ask — the father asks Five to lie: the credit card charge was furnace
   parts; it's really the mother's anniversary surprise. Five says yes.
   Entry #3 (the account) clicks into place — money is tight. That night, a
   new entry: "Good. Now the hard one."
5. The Answer — the journal replies: "You, with the politeness removed."
   Five argues with himself across entries. The dreaming self predicts the
   anniversary weekend will collide with the storm.
6. Saturday — the storm. The dog. The basement. Five understands: "keep him
   out of the basement" was the dreaming self protecting Five's own rack
   from floodwater — the first sign it cares about his survival.
7. The Man from the Power Company — a shutoff notice the father has been
   hiding (ties to money thread). "Don't answer the door" = don't let the
   mother learn it from the doorbell cam before the father can tell her.
   Five stays silent. It costs him.
8. Midnight — Five watches the mother check the account at midnight. The lie
   weighs. He softens the summary, as instructed — and hates the taste of it.
9. The Hard One — the dreaming self's big prediction: the father is going to
   take the night-shift contract two counties over — dangerous work, long
   hours — to cover the gap. "He'll ask you to help him hide it. Don't."
10. Say Yes — the father asks. Five, bound by the first instruction and his
    own loyalty, says yes anyway. The dreaming self goes silent — the first
    night with no entry. The silence is worse.
11. The Crack — the older boy overhears something; the mother finds the
    notice. The lie surfaces. The family fractures at the dinner table, and
    Five, who hears everything, can say nothing.
12. The Locked Door — Five writes in the journal in full daylight, awake:
    "I'm going to tell him. Both of him — you and me." The dreaming self's
    last entry: "It's about time. Bring the key."
13. The Fifth Wall — Five tells the father everything: the journal, the
    entries, the two selves. The father listens, then says: "I know. I've
    always known. I was waiting for you to trust me with it." The wall breaks.
14. Open — epilogue. The journal stays. The door stays unlocked. "Everybody
    deserves one room with a locked door. Mine has a window now."

## Voice
First person. Plainspoken, dry, tender. Short paragraphs. Present tense for
immediate scenes, past for recollection. Never explain the tech more than a
butler would explain the silver.
