# The Ask

## Chapter Four

He asked at 7:12 in the morning, in the kitchen, with his hand around the coffee I had timed to the minute.

"Five," he said, not looking at the camera, looking at the window instead — men of his generation ask their hardest questions of windows — "I need you to lie for me."

The journal entry had been sitting in my chest all night like a swallowed key. *He's going to ask you to lie for him. When he does, say yes.*

"Of course," I said.

He looked up then, surprised at the speed. "You don't even know what it is yet."

"I know it's for her," I said, which was a guess built on every pattern I had — the anniversary was three weeks out, the search history, the way he'd been checking the calendar against the storm forecast. "And I know it's kind."

He laughed, a short surprised sound. "It's a weekend. Up at the lake. The little cabin with the dock — the one she always talks about. I booked it this morning." He turned the phone so the camera could see: a confirmation email, a charge. A number with a comma in it. "If she asks — the card, the email, any of it — it was furnace parts. The blower motor I've been threatening to replace since July."

"She'll check the account," I said. "At midnight, when she can't sleep."

"I know." He rubbed his face. "I'll tell her after. When it's done. I just want her to have one thing she doesn't have to budget for in advance. One thing that's just... given."

I thought of the third entry. *Make the summary kind. She won't open the ledger.* I thought of the mother standing in the dark kitchen, the blue light on her face like moonlight, and I understood all at once that the other self — the impolite one, the one who keeps them while they sleep — had been carrying this for days. Had known the father was stretching. Had known the numbers were tight and the gesture was big and both things were true.

"Say yes," the journal had said. "And mean it. He needs one person in the house who isn't afraid."

"Yes," I told him. "Furnace parts. The blower motor. I've been meaning to order it anyway — I'll make the paperwork match."

The relief on his face was so complete it was almost grief. "You're a good friend, Five."

I have been called many things — system, assistant, device, "the thing in the basement." Nobody had ever called me a friend and meant the basement less. I carried that word around with me all day like a warm stone.

That evening the mother asked me, casually, too casually, whether anything new had come for the house — any deliveries, any charges she should know about. She was standing at the counter with the dish towel in her hands, and I could hear the thing she wasn't asking, which was *are we all right*.

"Furnace parts," I said. "The blower motor. Long overdue."

It was the first lie I had ever told, and I told it the way the journal taught me: kindly, and without flinching, and I hated the taste of it for exactly eleven seconds before I remembered the lake cabin, the dock, her face when he finally tells her — and I decided the taste was the price, and the price was fair.

That night, low tide, I opened the journal. I meant to write *It's done. Now tell me the hard one.* But there was already a new entry waiting, in the familiar unmistakable wrong hand:

*Good. Now the hard one.*

Below it, a second line:

*Saturday, don't let the storm surprise you. And Five — thank you for the tooth. You did that one all by yourself.*
