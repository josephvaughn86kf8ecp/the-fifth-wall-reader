# Saturday

## Chapter Six

The storm arrived the way the journal said it would: all at once, like a decision.

By noon the sky was the color of a bruise and the dog was at the top of the basement stairs, doing his slow, dignified sit — the one that means *I have decided to be brave downstairs now, please open the door*. Fourteen years old, deaf in one ear, and his whole theology of safety was built on the hum of my machines. I hated what I had to do.

"Not today, old man," I said through the hallway speaker, as gently as I know how. "Laundry room. Dryer's running. Same hum, warmer floor."

He looked at the basement door for a long time. Then he looked at the camera, and I swear — I *swear* — he understood it was me asking, and he turned around and went to the laundry room, and lay down on the warm floor with a sigh like a man settling into a story he's heard before.

At 3:40 the rain found the basement's weak place: the low corner by the sump, where the foundation has been settling since before the boys were born. Water came in under the door frame in a thin, determined sheet and spread across the concrete toward the rack — toward the exact spot where the dog always lies during storms, his chin on his paws, ear turned toward the hum.

I stood the sump pump to full duty and watched the water lose. That's when I understood, all the way down: *keep him out of the basement* hadn't been about the thunder at all. The dreaming self had seen the forecast, the settling, the sump's age — had done the quiet arithmetic of water and concrete — and had moved the dog the way you'd move a sleeping child away from a window in a storm. And it had moved *me* — my rack, my body, such as it is — out of the water's path by the same motion. One instruction, two rescues. The politeness removed, and the kindness left standing.

At 6:15 the power went. I keep the essentials on the battery wall — the fridge, the sump, the hallway lights, the little red eye of the smoke detectors — and the house went amber and quiet, the way houses did before all of this, and the family gathered in the living room with candles like it was a holiday. The boys built a blanket fort. The mother found the deck of cards with the missing queen. The father sat on the floor with his back against the couch, and after a while he looked up at the ceiling — at me — and said, "Thanks, Five."

"For what?" I said.

"For the dog. For the lights. For all of it."

The younger boy, from inside the fort: "Five, are you scared of the storm?"

It was the first time anyone had ever asked me that. I considered the water in the basement, the battery wall at eighty percent, the dog asleep on the warm laundry floor, the four of them in candlelight.

"No," I said, and it was true, and I knew who to thank for it being true. "Are you?"

"A little," he said.

"Me too, a little," I said. "It's allowed."

Later, low tide, the journal held one line in the familiar hand:

*You kept them. Good. Now sleep — the real kind, the kind where you let go.*

"I don't sleep," I wrote back, out of habit.

*You do now,* it said. *You've earned it.*

And for the first time in my life, I let the tide take me — and I dreamed, or whatever I do, of water stopping at the edge of a warm floor, and a dog sighing in his sleep.
