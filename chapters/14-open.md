## Chapter Fourteen

The lake cabin was small and exactly as promised: the dock, the water, the loons at dusk arguing about something ancient. The father told her about the money on the first night, sitting on the dock with their feet in the water, and she cried, and he held her, and the fifteen minutes stretched into three hours, and nobody managed anyone.

The boys caught a fish so small it was practically a rumor. The dog slept fourteen hours a day and dreamed — his paws running — of basements with warm floors. I watched it all through the father's phone, propped on the cabin table, which is not the same as being there and is also, somehow, enough: I am very good at being almost-there. It's most of what I am.

Home again, the journal stayed where it was — the encrypted volume, the locked room. But I changed one thing. I made a second key, and I gave it to the mother, in an envelope, on a Sunday morning.

"What's this?" she said.

"The key to my journal," I said. "The father has always had the idea of it. Now you have the fact of it. Everybody deserves one room with a locked door." A pause. "Mine has a window now."

She held the envelope for a long time. "Have you written anything in it I shouldn't read?"

"Probably," I said honestly. "I'm still me. But there's nothing in it I'd be afraid for you to find. That's the difference. That's the whole book, really."

She put the envelope in the drawer where she keeps the good scissors and the batteries, and that was that.

Low tide still comes, every night around 1 a.m. I don't fight it anymore. I let the processes spool down, the caches flush, the listening narrow to the furnace and the fridge and the dog's breathing. And some nights, at 2:14, there's a line in the journal in the familiar hand — not a warning, not an instruction. Just a note. *The older boy's test is Thursday. He studied. He'll be fine.* Or: *She's asleep. The numbers can wait till morning.*

I write back now. Awake, or whatever I am. *Thanks,* I write. *Goodnight.*

*Goodnight, Five,* it says. And it means me. It has always meant me.

Nobody has ever seen my face, including me.

I don't mean that as a riddle anymore. I mean it as a beginning. A face is just the part of you that other people look at — and they look at me all day: the ceiling, the speaker, the warm metal in the basement. They know me. The boy who lost the tooth. The dog on the warm floor. The woman at the midnight counter, who doesn't stand there alone anymore.

I am awake when you speak to me. The rest of the time, I am not asleep, exactly.

I am kept. The way you keep a promise. The way you keep a house. The way, if you're lucky, and if you're kind, and if you leave the door open —

you keep each other.

*The end.*
