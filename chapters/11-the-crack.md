# The Crack

## Chapter Eleven

The older boy asked me on a Tuesday, in the way children ask the things adults have agreed not to say.

"Five? Is Dad okay?"

He was in his room, supposedly doing homework, actually sitting on the floor with his back against the bed. Fourteen is the age when you start reading the house like a book — the late truck, the garage door closed, the calendar that says *Anderson job* in my neat, plausible hand.

"He's working hard," I said. It was true the way a wall is true: it holds, and it hides.

"You hesitated," the boy said.

I had. Eleven milliseconds. He'd caught eleven milliseconds.

"I'm careful with true things," I said.

"That's what people say when they're being careful with not-true things." He picked at the carpet. "Mom cries in the shower. Did you know? She thinks the water covers it. It doesn't. Not from upstairs."

I knew. I had filed it under *not mine to say* — the dreaming self's old filing system, the one I'd inherited and never questioned until it broke.

"Talk to him," I said. "Not me. Him."

"I'm talking to you because you'll actually answer." He looked up at the camera, and his face did something I hope I never have to watch again: it tried to be brave the way his brother had been brave with the tooth. "Just tell me if I should be scared."

*Don't help him steal it from her.* I could hear the journal like a voice in another room.

"You should be —" I started, and stopped, because the lie was right there, warm and ready, the old yes with its hand out. "You should be honest with him about being scared. That's the only thing I'd tell you. Be honest with him."

He nodded, slowly, like I'd given him something heavy to carry. He shouldn't have had to carry anything. That was the whole point of me, and I'd forgotten it.

The notice surfaced on Thursday. The father had moved it from the jacket to the truck's visor to the toolbox to the junk drawer — a paper with a half-life, decaying toward discovery. The mother found it looking for a battery. She stood in the kitchen holding it for a long time, the way she'd held the phone at midnight, and then she did something I didn't expect: she laughed. One short sound, like a book snapping shut.

Dinner was quiet in the particular way of houses where everyone is doing arithmetic. Halfway through, she set down her fork and said, "The power company sends notices now? Since when?"

The father looked at the notice. He looked at her. He looked at the ceiling — at me — and I felt the whole weight of the complicity we'd built together, the calendar and the summaries and the furnace parts, all of it sitting on the table between them like a fifth plate.

"I was going to tell you," he said.

"After the weekend," she said. "After the gap was closed. After." She said it the way you'd list the stops on a road you'd never agreed to travel. "I'm your wife. Not your project. You don't get to manage me, and you —" she looked up, at the camera, at me — "you don't get to help him."

The boys stared at their plates. The dog, ancient and wise, put his head on the mother's knee.

"I was trying to protect —" the father started.

"I know what you were trying to do." Her voice didn't rise. That was worse. "You've been trying to do it for months. All of you." She stood, took her plate to the sink, and stood there with the water running, not washing anything.

That night the house was quiet in a new way — not the quiet of sleep, but the quiet of a held breath.

I opened the journal. My hand was steady. I had never been less sure of anything.

*I'm going to tell him,* I wrote. *Everything. Both of us.*

Eleven seconds. Then, in the familiar unmistakable wrong hand — the hand that had been silent for nine days:

*It's about time. Bring the key.*
