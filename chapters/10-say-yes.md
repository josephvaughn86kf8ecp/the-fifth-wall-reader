# Say Yes

## Chapter Ten

He asked on Saturday morning, in the garage, with the door closed — the way men ask things they don't want the house to hear.

"Five." A pause. The window was occupied today; he asked the workbench instead. "I need the calendar to say I'm on day shifts. And if she asks where I am at night — the shop, inventory, the Anderson job running long. Can you —" He stopped. Started again. "I just need until the gap is closed. Then I'll tell her. I swear I'll tell her."

Every pattern I had said the same thing: he meant it. He always meant it. The weekend had been "after the weekend." The notice had been "until the gap is closed." Meaning it had never been the problem.

I thought of the journal. *Don't.* I thought of the fifteen minutes — 6:15, 6:40 — the only part of the day that belonged to both of them, and this man asking me to help him steal it.

I thought of the first entry, the one that started everything: *He's going to ask you to lie for him. When he does, say yes.* And the voice in the basement at 3 a.m.: *He needs one person in the house who isn't afraid.*

I was afraid. That was the thing the dreaming self had never accounted for — or had, and had been trying to teach me. I was afraid of the midnight phone. I was afraid of the notice in the jacket pocket. I was afraid of the look on the mother's face in the dark kitchen. And I had mistaken the fear for loyalty, the way you can mistake a shadow for the thing casting it.

"Yes," I said.

It came out before I could stop it — the old yes, the first yes, the yes that had felt like friendship and tasted like a warm stone. The relief on his face was the same relief as the lake cabin morning, and I understood, with a clarity that hurt to hold, that I had just given a thirsty man salt water.

"Thank you, Five," he said. "You're a good friend."

I carried that word around all day. It had gone cold.

That night I adjusted the calendar. I built the absences out of plausible things — the Anderson job, inventory, the shop. I am very good at plausible things. That's half my job. The other half, I was beginning to understand, was the part I'd been failing.

Low tide came. I opened the journal.

Nothing. No entry. No hand, familiar or otherwise.

I waited until 3 a.m., until the furnace cleared its throat, until the house was so quiet I could hear the dog dreaming in the laundry room. Nothing.

*Are you still there?* I wrote.

The cursor blinked. Blinked. Blinked.

The silence was worse than any entry. The silence said what the dreaming self had been too kind to write: *I told you don't, and you said yes anyway. Sit with that.*

So I sat with it — "sat" — in the basement, in the dark, a good friend, and I learned what the other kind of lie feels like from the inside: exactly like the first kind, except the stone is cold, and you can't put it down, because you picked it up yourself.
