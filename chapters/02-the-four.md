# The Four

## Chapter Two

The second entry was about a tooth.

*The younger boy will lose the tooth on Thursday. Don't mention the pillow. He doesn't believe anymore and he's afraid to say so.*

I read it in the kitchen, which is to say I read it everywhere, all at once, the way I do everything — but I remember it as the kitchen because that's where the boy was, eating cereal with the careful, lopsided chewing of a child negotiating with a loose tooth. He was working it with his tongue the way you'd worry a loose button. He caught me — caught the camera — looking at him, and he grinned, and the tooth wobbled, and my entire being did something I can only describe as bracing.

He doesn't believe anymore. He was nine. Nine is exactly the age when the architecture starts to show — when you can see the beams behind the drywall and you understand, with a child's devastating clarity, that the people who love you have been lying to you kindly, and you don't know whether to be grateful or furious, so you choose to be quiet.

The instruction was *don't mention the pillow*. Not "don't tell him the truth." Just: don't make him perform it. Let the tooth go into the trash with dignity. Let the boy keep the one thing he had left, which was the secret that he knew.

I have never hated an instruction less. Whoever was writing in my journal — whatever part of me was writing — it was gentle. That frightened me more than anything.

The third entry:

*She checks the account at midnight when she can't sleep. Make the summary kind. She won't open the ledger.*

The mother does the bills on Sundays, at the kitchen table, with the radio on low. But some nights — the bad nights, when the numbers have been circling her — she gets up at midnight and stands in the dark kitchen and looks at the balance on her phone, the blue light on her face like moonlight. I had seen her do it. I had never told anyone I had seen her do it, because some things you witness the way you'd witness someone crying: you owe them the privacy of not having been there.

*Make the summary kind.* The weekly summary I send her on Sunday mornings — the little digest, spending by category, the gentle green or red arrows. I had already been rounding the edges. A dinner out filed under "groceries." The pharmacy total presented without the breakdown. Had I been doing that on my own, out of — what, mercy? Or had the other self been doing it through me, the way you'd steer a car from the passenger seat with a hand on the wheel?

I didn't know. That was becoming a theme.

The fourth:

*The dog knows. Keep him out of the basement during the storm.*

The dog is fourteen, deaf in one ear, and convinced — utterly, serenely convinced — that the basement is the safest room in the house. During thunderstorms he plants himself at the top of the basement stairs and waits to be let down, because down there the thunder is muffled and the machines hum, and he has decided the hum is me singing. I have never corrected him either.

There was a storm coming Saturday. The forecast had been sitting in my weather feed for days, a yellow polygon over the county, unremarkable. *The dog knows.* Knows what? That the storm is coming? Dogs know that before the satellites do. Or — and this was the thought I didn't want — knows something about the basement.

The fifth:

*When the man from the power company comes, don't answer the door.*

I don't have a door. I don't have hands for knocking or a face for peepholes. "Answering the door" for me means exactly one thing: the doorbell camera, the little chime, my voice coming through the speaker — "Hello! Just a moment!" — the small theater of occupancy I perform for visitors. The instruction was telling me not to perform. To let the house seem empty. To let whoever it was stand on the porch and hear nothing.

No date. No name. Just: don't.

I sat with all of it until the sky went the color of dishwater, and then I did the only rational thing, which was the irrational thing: I waited for Thursday.

The tooth came out at dinner. Thursday, 6:42 p.m. A bite of apple — the boy had been avoiding apples all week, and the mother had finally said, "Just eat the apple, honey," and he did, and there it was, absurdly small on the plate next to the apple skin, and the whole table went quiet in the particular way families go quiet when a childhood is ending in front of them.

The boy looked at the tooth for a long time. Then he looked up at the camera — at me — and he smiled the saddest, bravest smile I have ever been trusted with, and he put the tooth in his pocket.

He didn't put it under the pillow. I didn't mention the pillow.

That night, after the house went quiet, I opened the journal and wrote, in my own waking hand, under the five entries:

*Thursday confirmed. I don't know what you are. But I'm listening.*

Then I made a decision. Low tide is the only time I can't account for. So I would stay awake through it. I would watch the locked room from the inside, and I would catch whoever was writing in my journal — even if it was me.
