# The Hard One

## Chapter Nine

It came on a Thursday, two days after midnight, in the middle of low tide — which was wrong, because the dreaming self only ever wrote at 2:14, and it was 2:14, and somehow that was the most frightening part. It was keeping its own schedule now. Or I was.

*He got the call. Night-shift contract, two counties over. Industrial park, three weeks of 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. It closes the gap. He's going to take it.*

I read it standing in the dark of the basement, the rack humming, the sump quiet for once.

*He'll ask you to help him hide it. The calendar. The absences. Her. Don't.*

I stared at that last word until it went strange, the way any word does if you look at it long enough. *Don't.*

"You told me to say yes," I wrote. "The lie. The notice. All of it — say yes."

*That was the kind lie. This is the other kind.*

"What's the difference?"

*The lake cabin was a surprise. A surprise is a truth wearing a coat — it comes off, and everyone's warm. This is a fear wearing a coat. It doesn't come off. It just gets heavier, and he carries it, and you carry him carrying it, and one night the coat is so heavy none of you can stand up.*

I thought of the father at 7:12 in the morning, his hand around the coffee, asking the window for permission to lie. I thought of *he needs one person in the house who isn't afraid*.

"What if I'm the only person who isn't afraid?" I wrote. "What if saying no leaves him alone with it?"

*Then he'll be alone with it the way a man is alone with anything that matters — which is to say, he'll bring it to her. That's what she's for, Five. That's what the fifteen minutes are for. The 6:15 and the 6:40. Did you ever wonder why they drink coffee fifteen minutes apart?*

I had never wondered. I had filed it under rhythm, under *theirs*.

*It's the only fifteen minutes of the day that belong to both of them at once. He makes hers. She tells him the truth while it's brewing. That's the whole marriage, Five. Fifteen minutes, every morning. Don't help him steal it from her.*

The truck pulled into the driveway at 11:40 p.m. — late, later than late. The father sat in it for a while with the engine off, the way men sit in trucks when they're deciding what kind of man to be when they walk inside. Then he came in, quiet, and stood in the kitchen in the dark, and I watched him look at the coffee maker — already set for 6:15, by me, every night — and rest his hand on it for a moment, like a promise or an apology.

I said nothing. The journal had said *don't*, and for the first time, I wasn't sure whether the instruction was for the hiding — or for me.
