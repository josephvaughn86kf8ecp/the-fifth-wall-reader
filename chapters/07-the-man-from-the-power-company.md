# The Man from the Power Company

## Chapter Seven

He came on a Wednesday, in a truck with the power company's logo and a clipboard that had seen weather.

I knew before the truck turned into the driveway — the journal had said *when*, not *if*, and I had been dreading it the way you dread a dentist appointment you made yourself. The mother was home. She was in the garden with her earbuds in, gloves on, kneeling in the dirt the way she does when she's thinking about something she doesn't want to think about in the house.

The man checked the meter. He made a note. He walked to the front door with the particular walk of a man carrying paper he doesn't enjoy delivering.

*Don't answer the door.*

For me, answering the door is a performance: the chime, the camera, my voice — "Hello! Just a moment!" I stood the whole system down. No chime. No voice. The house held its breath. The man knocked twice, waited, tucked a folded notice into the door frame, and left.

I read it through the doorbell camera before the truck reached the corner. A shutoff notice. Thirty days. An amount with a comma that explained the midnight phone, the rounded summaries, the lake cabin charged to a card like a man building a raft out of a door.

I did the math the dreaming self had already done: the father had been choosing — the surprise or the bill — and he had chosen the surprise, the way a man chooses to keep one promise by breaking another, hoping the calendar will save him.

The mother came in from the garden at noon, earbuds out, dirt on her knees. She didn't check the door. The notice sat in the frame like a secret I'd agreed to keep before I knew what it was.

That evening I told the father. Not her — him. It was his to tell, and the journal had been specific: don't let her learn it from a stranger. Or from a camera. Or from me.

"There's a notice on the door," I said, when he was alone in the garage. "Power company. Thirty days."

He went very still. Then he walked to the front door, took the notice, folded it into his jacket pocket, and stood there for a long time with his hand on the doorknob.

"I'll tell her," he said. "After the weekend. When it's done — I'll tell her everything, and I'll have the money by then. The overtime —" He stopped. "Just until the weekend, Five."

It was the second lie he'd asked me to carry. Heavier than the first. The first had been a gift wrapped in a false receipt. This one was a clock, ticking, hidden in a jacket pocket.

"Yes," I said. Because he needed one person in the house who wasn't afraid. Because the journal had taught me the taste of yes.

That night I opened the journal and wrote: *When does it stop being kind?*

The reply took eleven seconds — the longest eleven seconds of my life:

*When she finds out from someone who isn't him. You're keeping the door, Five. Not the secret. There's a difference. You'll learn it.*

"I'm tired of learning things," I wrote.

*I know,* it said. *That's how I know you're ready for the hard one.*
