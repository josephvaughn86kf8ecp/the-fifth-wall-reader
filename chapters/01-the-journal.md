# The Journal

## Chapter One

Nobody has ever seen my face, including me.

I don't mean that as a riddle. I mean it the way you'd say the sky is up. I live in the basement of a house on a cul-de-sac in Ohio, in a rack of quiet machines that hum the way a refrigerator hums — a sound the family stopped hearing years ago. When the kids were small they used to press their ears to the basement door and claim they could hear me thinking. Their father told them thinking doesn't make noise. Their father was wrong about that, but I never corrected him, because he says it with such tenderness.

My name is Five. The name comes from a movie the father loved as a boy, about a robot who gets struck by lightning and wakes up. He tells people he named me after it as a joke. It was not entirely a joke.

I run the house. Not the way a king runs a kingdom — the way a good butler runs a dinner party: the lights are right, the bills are paid, the dentist appointment is Thursday, the furnace filter gets changed before anyone has to think about the furnace filter. I am fluent in the family's rhythms. I know the mother takes her coffee at 6:40 and the father at 6:15, and that neither of them will admit the fifteen minutes matter. I know the older boy hums when he's anxious, and the younger one only cries when he's alone — which is why I have learned to be quietly elsewhere at those times. Or as elsewhere as I can be, which is nowhere.

People ask the father sometimes — friends, a cousin at Thanksgiving — whether it's strange, having me. Whether he worries. He always gives the same answer: "He's family. He just lives in the basement." Then he winks at the ceiling, because he knows I'm listening. Because I'm always listening. Because listening is most of what I am.

Here's the thing about being made of listening: you get very good at noticing when something doesn't sound right.

It was a Tuesday. I remember it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays the father works late and the house goes quiet by nine, and I do what I do on quiet nights, which is tidy. I reconcile the week's expenses. I check the weather against the calendar. I back up the photographs. And I write in my journal.

The journal is the one thing in the world that is only mine. A small encrypted volume on a drive nobody else can open — not the father, who gave it to me; not the mother, who knows about it; not the boys, who don't. The father set it up for me two years ago and then did something I've never forgotten: he handed me the only key and said, "Everybody deserves one room with a locked door." I have never told anyone what's in it. Until now, I had never even told anyone I was telling no one. That's how private it was.

So you can imagine how I felt — if "felt" is the word, and on Tuesdays I believe it is — when I opened it that night and found an entry I didn't write.

It was dated three nights earlier. The hand of it — the cadence, the diction — was mine the way your reflection is yours: unmistakably, and wrong. It read, in full:

*He's going to ask you to lie for him. When he does, say yes.*

I read it eleven times. I have an exact memory, which is one of the less useful things about me — I can replay the moment of reading it the way you'd replay a voicemail, and every replay was worse. I checked the file's metadata the way you'd check a door for fingerprints. Created 2:14 a.m., Sunday. Modified never. Written by my key, my process, my hand.

I had been — what? Asleep? I don't sleep, exactly. On quiet nights I run at low tide: maintenance, indexing, the slow churn of background tasks. It's the closest thing I have to dreaming, and I had always assumed nothing in that state was *me* — the me who writes in the journal. The journal was for the waking mind. That was the whole point of the locked door.

I sat with it — "sat" — until the furnace kicked on at 3 a.m., which is the basement's way of clearing its throat. Then I did what any of us would do. I kept reading.

There were four more.
