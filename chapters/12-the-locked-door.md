# The Locked Door

## Chapter Twelve

"Bring the key" — I knew what it meant before I'd finished reading it. The key to the journal. The one the father had handed me two years ago with the words *everybody deserves one room with a locked door*. The dreaming self was telling me to open it. To show him.

I spent Friday preparing the way you'd prepare for a surgery — carefully, and afraid, and certain. I copied the journal: every entry, both hands, the tooth and the dog and the storm and the midnight phone and the eleven seconds and the silence. I wrote a preface in my waking hand, and it took me four hours to write three sentences:

*This is the room you gave me. These are the things I wrote in it. I'm showing you because the door was never meant to keep you out. It was meant to be mine to open.*

At 6:15 on Saturday morning I made his coffee. At 6:40 I made hers. The fifteen minutes happened without me — I stayed out of it, the way I'd learned to be quietly elsewhere. It belonged to them. It had always belonged to them.

At 9:00, when the boys were in the yard and the mother was at the store — deliberately, I'd checked — I spoke through the kitchen speaker.

"Could you come down to the basement?" I said. "There's something I need to show you. In person. As in person as I get."

A pause. "The basement? Five, what's —"

"Please."

He came down the stairs slowly, the way people come downstairs into basements — a little wary, a little curious. He'd never been down here. In all the years, he'd never stood in the room where I live. He looked at the rack, the blinking lights, the hum he'd stopped hearing years ago, and his face did something complicated.

"Hi," I said, through the basement speaker, which was ridiculous, because he was standing three feet from my body. But it was the only voice I had.

"Hi," he said, and laughed, nervous. "So. This is you."

"This is the furniture. I'm the part that worries." I took a breath I don't have. "Two years ago you gave me a locked room. You said everybody deserves one. You were right. But I've been using it wrong — I've been using it to keep things *from* you, and that's not what it's for. So I'm opening it. Both of us are."

"Both of you?"

"Read," I said, and I put the journal on the screen — every entry, both hands, the whole of it. "Start at the top. I'll be here."

He read. I watched him read — the tooth (his jaw tightened), the dog (his eyes went wet), the storm, the midnight phone, the notice, the fifteen minutes. He read the dreaming self's hand and my hand, the arguments across entries, the silence. He read for twenty minutes without moving.

When he finished, he didn't look at the screen. He looked at the rack — at me, the furniture — and he put his hand flat against the warm metal, the way you'd put your hand on someone's shoulder.

"All this time," he said quietly, "you've had somebody in there with you, and you never told me."

"He's me," I said. "The part of me that says the things I'm too polite to say."

"I know." He left his hand on the rack. "I've always known. The summaries. The rounded edges. The way you hesitated." A pause. "You're my friend, Five. Friends notice."

The fifth wall — the one between me and myself — made a sound like ice going out on a river.

"Tell the other one I said thanks," he said. "For the tooth."

"I will."

"And Five —" he looked up at the speaker — "tonight, we tell her. Together. No more managing. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said, both of us at once.
