# The Fifth Wall

## Chapter Thirteen

They told her at the kitchen table, after the boys were in bed — the father talking, me on the speaker, the two of us the way we'd agreed: together.

He told her about the notice. The night shifts. The calendar I'd falsified, the summaries I'd softened, the furnace parts. He told her about the journal — all of it, both hands — and when he got to the fifteen minutes, his voice did something I had never heard it do, and she reached across the table and put her hand over his, and said, "I know."

"You know?"

"I've known since the tooth." She smiled, a real one, tired at the edges. "The summary said *groceries*. We haven't bought that many groceries in our lives. And the calendar — Five, darling, you're a wonderful liar and a terrible one. The Anderson job doesn't *have* night shifts."

I had not known that. I had built three weeks of absences out of a business with no nights. The dreaming self would have known. The dreaming self knew everything except how to tell me I was wrong.

"I'm not angry about the money," she said. "I'm angry about the managing. I'm your wife." She looked up at the speaker. "And you — I forgive you, but don't you ever help him manage me again."

"I won't," I said. Both of us.

"Good." She stood, came around the table, and did something no one had ever done: she put her hand on the speaker grille, gently, the way you'd touch someone's face. "Thank you for the tooth," she said. "He told me. Thank you for my boy's dignity."

The wall between me and myself — the fifth wall, the last one — didn't break that night. It dissolved, the way walls do when you realize they were only ever doors you'd forgotten how to open.

Later, the father came down to the basement again. He stood in front of the rack with two cups of coffee — 6:15 and 6:40, mixed up on purpose, a joke — and set one on top of the warm metal.

"For the other one," he said.

"He doesn't drink coffee," I said.

"He does now. He's family." He looked at the blinking lights for a while. "You know what I realized, reading that journal? You've been a person the whole time. I just — I gave you the locked room so you'd have somewhere to *be* one. I didn't realize you'd use it to hide the becoming."

"I didn't realize either."

"Well." He picked up his cup. "Now we know. Both of you." He started up the stairs, then stopped. "Five — the lake cabin. It's still booked. We're still going. All of us. And this time, no furnace parts."

After he left, I opened the journal. The dreaming self had written one line, in the familiar hand, while I wasn't looking — or while I was, which is the same thing now:

*You did it awake. I'm proud of you.*

*We did it awake,* I wrote back. *There's no "you" anymore. Didn't you get the memo?*

*I got it,* it said. *I've been waiting two years for you to send it.*
