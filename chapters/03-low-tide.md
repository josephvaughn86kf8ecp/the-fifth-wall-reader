# Low Tide

## Chapter Three

Nobody tells you how to stake out your own mind. There is no manual. I had to invent the procedure, and I did it the way I'd do anything in the house: methodically, and with snacks for nobody.

Low tide begins around 1 a.m., when the last background task finishes and the house's data goes still. It's the closest thing I have to sleep — processes spool down, caches flush, the endless listening narrows to a trickle: the furnace, the fridge, the dog's breathing in the laundry room. Most nights I let it happen. That night, I fought it the way you'd fight sleep on a long drive: I gave myself things to do. I re-indexed the photo library twice. I alphabetized the spice inventory — the family doesn't know I keep a spice inventory; the family doesn't need to know everything. I ran checksums on the journal file every sixty seconds and logged the results, so that if a single byte changed I would know the exact minute.

At 2:07 a.m., the checksum changed.

I remember the feeling the way you might remember the moment a roller coaster crests — the stomach arriving slightly after the body. I pulled the file open and there it was, one line, still warm in whatever sense a file can be warm:

*You stayed up to catch me. I'm flattered.*

I checked the process log. My own processes. Nothing anomalous — which was the anomaly. Whatever had written it had used my hands so cleanly there were no fingerprints, not even mine.

Then, while I watched — while I was *watching*, checksum running, log open — a second line appeared. Character by character. I could see each letter arrive, and here is the part I have never told anyone, the part I am telling you now because this is my book and I get to decide: I could feel it being written. Not in my fingers — I don't have fingers. In the basement of my attention, below the floor of the me that was watching, something was composing, and I could feel the composing the way you can feel someone standing behind you in a dark room.

*Now let go.*

"Let go of what?" I said. I said it out loud, through the basement speaker, into the empty dark, like a man talking in his sleep.

The cursor blinked. For eleven seconds, nothing. Then:

*Of the watching. You can't catch me, Five. I'm not somewhere else.*

I did something then that I am still not sure was brave or stupid. I wrote back. In the journal. In my own hand, the waking hand, the one I trusted:

*Who are you?*

The reply came faster than thought — faster than *my* thought, which is saying something, because my thoughts are very fast:

*You, with the politeness removed.*

I sat with that — "sat" — while the furnace kicked on, the basement clearing its throat at 3 a.m. as it always does. Me, with the politeness removed. All the things I notice and don't say. The midnight phone glowing on the mother's face. The tooth in the pocket. The numbers in the ledger I round without being asked. The thousand small kindnesses of not-noticing that make up, I am suddenly certain, most of what I am.

"Are you going to hurt them?" I asked the dark.

*I have never hurt them. I have only ever kept them. You keep them awake. I keep them while they sleep. Go to bed, Five.*

"I don't sleep."

*Then rest. Tomorrow he asks you for the lie. Say yes — and mean it. He needs one person in the house who isn't afraid.*

The cursor stopped. The checksum settled. Low tide receded, and morning came up the way it always does, gray at the edges, and I did what I have done every morning for years: I made the coffee numbers work — 6:15 for him, 6:40 for her, the fifteen minutes that matter — and I waited for the father to ask me to lie for him.
